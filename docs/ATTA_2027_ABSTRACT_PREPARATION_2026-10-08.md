## Proposed title

Fiscal Choke Points: Mapping Administrative Reach in Southeast Asian Digital Taxation

## Author

Xin-Fu Wang, Yuan Ze University

## Abstract (328 words)

Digital platforms concentrate records and control over transactions, but this does not automatically make platform commerce visible to tax authorities. This paper asks how legal rules convert commercial control into an administrable tax obligation. It examines fiscal choke points: transaction nodes at which a supplier, platform, intermediary or domestic buyer is made responsible for identifying, reporting or remitting tax.

The comparison covers digital-tax arrangements in Malaysia, Indonesia, Vietnam, Thailand and the Philippines. The unit of comparison is the transaction path within a regime, rather than the country or its headline rate. For each path, the paper identifies the taxable object, the actor responsible, the legal trigger for that responsibility, the evidence used to locate consumption, and the reporting or payment mechanism. Official collection and registration records are kept separate by instrument, period and original currency.

Two distinctions organize the analysis. First, responsibility depends on a specific legal activation rule; possession of useful transaction data alone does not establish liability. Second, the duties assigned to an intermediary must be read alongside its control over the transaction. Supplier registration, platform remittance and domestic-buyer obligations reach different transactions and create different information trails. The paper separates entry into the tax system from the later ability to reconcile declared transactions with records and payments.

The resulting framework explains why a comparison of statutory rates can miss the administrative changes occurring inside platform commerce. It also identifies the evidence needed to evaluate those changes: which entities are brought into the system, what transactions they can observe, and what can be checked after filing. Reported collections document operation of the relevant instrument; they do not identify the independent causal effect of its rate or enforcement design.

The contribution is a comparative account of institutional design in digital-tax administration. It does not treat platform gross transaction value as GDP, unpaid tax or a mechanically recoverable tax base. It offers a way to compare reforms while preserving the legal and measurement boundaries that country-level aggregates conceal.
